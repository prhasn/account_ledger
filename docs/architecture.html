In-Memory Account Ledger: Architecture and Trade-offs

Append-only, value-dated, deterministic replay

The ledger is a deterministic event replay. Source events and ledger entries are immutable, authorization state changes are appended, and every balance view is a projection that can be rebuilt. Money is held in integer minor units. The design is intentionally small enough to explain end to end, but three production concerns sit just outside the six-day window: scale, the operational meaning of a value date, and the full authorization lifecycle.

1. Append-only at scale

The current design keeps every event, every ledger entry, every authorization transition, every daily snapshot, every accrual, and every error in memory. The append-only log is the source of truth. The authorization map and the per-day snapshots are indexes, not authority. That separation is the right starting point, but it is also where the first 100x pressure appears.

At 100x volume, arithmetic is not the first bottleneck. The first break is the combination of an unbounded in-memory history and an O(n) as-of balance query. Each day close asks for an as-of balance per account, and each restatement asks for a family of as-of balances across earlier days. With enough events, those scans dominate the work. Memory grows with total history rather than with the current working set: the event log, the transition log, closed authorizations, daily snapshots, and restatement maps are all retained for the life of the process. The second break is operational: a single-writer total order is a correctness feature, but it does not survive concurrent producers, retries, or late arrivals without an idempotency and ordering layer.

The cheapest structural change is a per-account, value-date cumulative balance index with periodic checkpoints, backed by a segmented append-only log. A checkpoint stores the account balance at a known sequence and value date. The index answers an as-of query in logarithmic time, or in constant time from the nearest checkpoint. Old log segments can leave RAM and live in object storage without changing the source-of-truth contract. Only active holds need to stay in the hot authorization map; terminal authorizations can be archived with their transitions. This is cheaper than redesigning the ledger as a distributed system, and it defers both the query and the memory problem. The next change would be an ingress idempotency key and a durable single-writer partition per account, because duplicate or out-of-order postings are the first correctness failure at volume, not arithmetic overflow.

Where state grows without bound: the event log, the authorization transition log, the daily snapshot list, the accrual list, and the error list. The cheapest deferral is checkpoint plus value-date index plus segmented storage, not a bigger heap.

2. Value-dated entries in production

For a UAE-licensed bank, a value date is not a display detail. It drives when funds are economically available, how a customer statement is explained, which reporting date a balance belongs to, and how fee and interest calculations are justified. A post that arrives after the business-day cut-off but carries an earlier value date creates a difference between the as-at close the bank already reported and the as-of view it can produce later. That difference is the operational surface: end-of-day cut-offs, backdating windows, restatement reports, customer statements, dispute evidence, audit trails, reconciliation to the general ledger, and the IFRS 9 and regulatory reporting feeds that consume historical balances.

The architecture resolves the accounting question with a deliberate policy: fees and interest are assessed at each close on the ledger known at that close, and a later backdated entry changes current and future closes and the restatement view, not the already-booked fee or accrual. This is defensible because a close is a point-in-time measurement. It is not sufficient as a production control, because the customer and the regulator can still see a restated history.

The one control I would add before go-live is a maker-checker backdating gate with a hard window and an immutable restatement register. Any entry whose value date is earlier than the current business date would require a reason code and a second approver, and any entry older than a configured window would be blocked outright. Posting the entry would automatically produce a restatement report showing every affected daily close and every fee or interest decision that was not reopened. That single control gives operations a bounded window, gives audit a named decision, and gives the customer-facing team the evidence it needs when a statement changes. It does not solve every value-date question, but it prevents silent backdating from becoming the default.

3. Authorization lifecycle

An authorization is a state machine, not a single approval call. A matching settlement is only one ending. Every other ending must release the hold atomically, append a transition with a reason, and leave the ledger unchanged. The production rule should be that no hold can disappear without a transition that names its ending.

Ending Real-world scenario Mandated system behavior
Time expiry A hotel or car-rental pre-authorization is never captured. Release the remaining hold at the configured expiry, append EXPIRED, and post no ledger entry. Notify the customer where required.
Merchant void or cancellation An order is cancelled before capture. Release the hold, append CANCELLED with the merchant reference, and make the amount available immediately. The operation is idempotent.
Customer or bank cancellation Lost card, confirmed fraud, or a customer request to stop the purchase. Release the hold, append CANCELLED with a reason code, and require maker-checker for a bank-initiated cancellation.
Partial settlement A restaurant bill or final capture is lower than the original estimate. Debit the actual amount, release the remainder, append SETTLED_PARTIAL, and retain the original authorized amount for audit.
Over-settlement A tip or currency adjustment pushes the capture above the hold. Apply a stated policy: reject the excess, or debit the excess only if available balance supports it. Never silently exceed the hold.
Duplicate settlement A network retry or a merchant double-capture. Use a settlement idempotency key. The second attempt is a no-op with an audit record, not a second debit.
Account closure The account is closed while holds are still active. Cancel every active hold, append CANCELLED, and block new authorizations. Do not close the account with an unexplained hold.
Decline at creation Available balance is insufficient. Create a DECLINED authorization record with no hold. This is a business outcome, not an error.
Orphaned hold A process crash or message loss between hold and settlement. A recovery job reconciles the authorization network against local state, expires or reopens the hold, and appends the reconciliation reason.
Manual operations adjustment Fraud investigation, dispute, or a goodwill release. Require maker-checker, a reason code, and an append-only transition. Never edit the original authorization record.
Settlement after expiry A late capture arrives after the hold was released. Reject the settlement, retain the EXPIRED state, and require a new authorization or an explicitly approved unauthenticated debit.
Chargeback after settlement Fraud or a customer dispute after funds moved. This is not an authorization ending. Handle it as a separate dispute and compensating-entry process with its own evidence and approval.

The invariant is that available balance is ledger balance minus active holds, and that every hold release is an appended transition. The UI, the API, and the reconciliation job all read the same projection, so none of them can silently disagree about whether funds are reserved.

4. What I cut and why

The scope was deliberately narrow: no web layer, no persistence, no database, no UI. Every simplification below is a production risk deferred, not a claim that the risk does not exist.

Simplification Reason Production risk deferred
In-memory only The brief excludes persistence and the suite is a replay. Process loss destroys the ledger and its audit trail. No replication, no backup, no retention policy, no disaster recovery.
Single-threaded replay Deterministic ordering makes the result explainable. Concurrent postings can double-spend, race a hold against a settlement, or interleave a reversal with a fee. A durable single-writer partition or a serializable log is required.
No ingress idempotency store The scenario has unique event ids. A network retry can duplicate a credit, debit, fee, or settlement. Duplicate settlement is a direct customer harm.
No authorization expiry or over-settlement workflow Neither case is exercised by the six-day window. Stale holds can freeze customer funds. A late or larger capture has no safe policy. The lifecycle table above is the production mandate, not the build.
No FX or multi-currency conversion Each account has one currency and the brief supplies no rate. Cross-currency reporting, fee currency, and settlement currency become ambiguous. Adding an implicit rate would be worse than refusing the case.
Fixed daily interest and no holiday calendar The brief gives a daily rate and six abstract days. Actual/365, actual/360, business-day conventions, leap years, and cut-off times can change every accrual. A production ledger must state its convention.
Closed-period finality for fees and interest It is deterministic and avoids retroactive liability. A late backdated entry can make a customer dispute a historical fee or interest amount. The restatement gate and customer remediation process are missing.
No tax, withholding, fee waiver, or refund workflow Out of scope for the ledger core. Regulatory deductions and customer remediation become manual and unaudited. A refund must be a new append-only event, never a mutation.
No reconciliation or regulatory reporting The suite reports to a console, not to a bank. General-ledger breaks, IFRS 9 feeds, and regulatory returns have no control total. The value-date restatement report is the first missing control.
No API, authentication, PII handling, or observability The assessment is a local core, not a service. No security boundary, no access audit, no metrics, no tracing, and no alerting. These are go-live blockers, not polish.

The detailed numbers, ambiguities, and rejected criteria are in the repository's NUMBERS.md, AMBIGUITIES.md, and REJECTED.md.
